      How Standard US Restaurant Tip-Out Percentages Work

      At the end of a shift, servers rarely keep 100% of their credit card and cash tips. You share a set cut with the support team that turned your tables, ran your plates, and poured your drinks. Most US dining rooms run on one of two models: a percentage of your net sales (the standard in casual and upscale dining) or a percentage of your total tips.

      Always check whether your checkout report lists gross sales or net sales. Gross sales include comps, voids, and discounts. Net sales reflect what guests actually paid before tax, which is why fair houses base server tip-out on net sales and separate liquor sales for the bar.

      Support Role % of Sales Model % of Tips Model What It Covers
      Bartender 5% – 10% of liquor sales 5% – 10% of tips Service well drinks, wine bottles, and draft pours
      Busser 1% – 2% of net sales 10% – 15% of tips Clearing plates, wiping tables, refilling water
      Food Runner 1% – 2% of net/food sales 5% – 10% of tips Delivering hot dishes and running sides from the pass
      Host / Expo 0.5% – 1% of net sales 2% – 5% of tips Managing table rotation, plating checks, ticket pacing

      How to Run a Fair Bartender Tip Pool

      Behind a busy bar, splitting tips evenly per head only works if everyone clocks in and closes together. When an opener leaves at 8 PM and a closer stays until 2 AM, an hourly split or point-weighted pool keeps checkout transparent and stops arguments before they start.

      1. 1

        Combine the Full Pot

        Count the cash tip jar, add credit card tips from the bar POS readout, and include server bar tip-outs if your house pools them together.

      2. 2

        Total the Hours or Points

        Add up every shift's worked hours for a straight hourly split, or assign fixed weights (like 1.0 for bartenders and 0.5 for a barback).

      3. 3

        Multiply and Balance Cents

        Divide the pool by total hours or points, multiply by each person's share, and distribute any leftover cents to the highest remainders so the drawer balances to the penny.

      Quick Friday Bar Example: Say the bar brings in $900.00 total across three people working 8, 6.5, and 5.5 hours (20 hours combined). The hourly tip rate is $45.00/hr. The 8-hour bartender walks with $360.00, the 6.5-hour mid gets $292.50, and the 5.5-hour closer gets $247.50.       End-of-Shift Tip-Out FAQ

      Should server tip-out be based on gross sales or net sales?
      Most US restaurants calculate server tip-out on net sales, not gross sales. Net sales remove sales tax, comps, and voids so you are not tipping out support staff on discounted or canceled items. Always check your POS checkout report to see which line your house uses.
      What is a normal restaurant tip-out percentage for bussers, food runners, and bartenders?
      On a sales-based system, typical US rates run 5% to 10% of liquor and bar sales to the bartender, 1% to 2% of net sales to bussers, 1% to 2% of net or food sales to food runners, and 0.5% to 1% to hosts or expo. On a tips-based system, servers usually tip out 20% to 35% of total tips split across support roles.
      How do you split a bartender tip pool when people work different hours?
      Add up every bartender's hours on the shift to get total hours worked. Divide the total tip pool (cash plus credit card tips) by total hours to find the hourly tip rate, then multiply that rate by each bartender's individual hours.
      Can managers or kitchen staff be included in a restaurant tip pool?
      Under US federal FLSA rules, managers and supervisors can never take money from a tip pool, even if they help pour drinks or run food. Back-of-house staff like line cooks and dishwashers can only be included in a tip pool if every employee in the pool earns at least the full federal minimum wage without a tip credit. State laws can be stricter.
